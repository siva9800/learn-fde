## 8.1 The brief

It is Thursday evening of week one at Godavari. You have the COO's seven candidates, the tracker
from Lakshmi's screen, the monthly report, and Thursday's counts with Srinivas and two analysts.
The COO has given you twenty minutes on Friday. You owe the engagement folder two things:

1. **The scored catalogue**, as data: one row per candidate with the evidence, the six scores and
   the three gates, in a CSV the chapter's script ranks. Data rather than prose so that the next
   checkpoint can rerun it and so that a reviewer can argue with one cell at a time.
2. **The problem selection memo**: the one-sentence problem statement, the catalogue, the gates,
   the scores with reasons, why the winner, the "not now, because, and instead" line for every
   other candidate, and the trap check. This is the first page of the engagement folder and the
   first draft of page one of Day 10's discovery document.

Code is light on purpose. The judgement is in the scores and the memo; the script applies the
gates, checks the scores against the evidence, and tells you whether the winner survives other
weightings.

**Files:**

| Path | What |
|---|---|
| [`worksheet/problem-selection-memo-template.md`](worksheet/problem-selection-memo-template.md) | the empty memo: eight sections, one table each |
| [`worksheet/godavari-problem-selection-memo.md`](worksheet/godavari-problem-selection-memo.md) | the filled Godavari memo (the answer to part B) |

Run on **Python 3.14.3** (October 2026), standard library only, from the chapter folder:

```bash
python code/score_candidates.py data/godavari-candidates.csv
```

It finishes in about a quarter of a second. No packages, no downloads, no API keys. The output is
plain ASCII, so no `-X utf8` is needed.

## 8.2 Part A: fill the catalogue

One row per candidate. The columns, and the rule for each:

| Column | Rule |
|---|---|
| `code`, `candidate`, `raised_by` | C1, C2, ...; a name that is a cost, not a product category; who raised it and in which conversation |
| `volume_per_month`, `hours_per_unit` | numbers with a source in the note; a guess is allowed if the note says it is a guess |
| `frequency`, `pain`, `measurability`, `data`, `blast_radius`, `sponsorship` | 1 to 5 against section 04's anchors; blast radius 5 is the worst (the script inverts it) |
| `has_baseline`, `has_data_owner`, `operator_wants_it` | yes or no; "probably" is no |
| `note` | what the floor said, in one line; the source of the volume and the hours |

Fill it before you read the chapter's version. The anchors in section 04 are the contract: two
people scoring the same row should land within a point. Where you and the chapter's CSV differ by
two or more, one of you has read the evidence differently, and that difference is the lesson.

## 8.3 Part B: run the script

`code/score_candidates.py` does five things in order: loads and validates the CSV (every score 1 to
5, every gate yes or no, every row with a code and a name); runs the consistency checks (a high
pain score with below-median hours, a high measurability score with no baseline, a high data score
with no owner, a blast radius of 5 on a candidate that passes); splits the candidates into those
that pass all three gates and those that do not; ranks the passing ones by weighted score with
ties going to more hours; and tries twelve other weightings to see whether the winner changes.

```text
== godavari-candidates.csv: 8 candidates, 3 pass all three gates ==

rank  code candidate                                  score  hours/mo   rupees/mo  raised by
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
   1  C1   Loan-file intake and completeness check       93     2,100    6.3 lakh  COO (kickoff)
   2  C2   Income verification from bank statements      67       675    2.0 lakh  COO (kickoff)
   3  C7   Credit-memo drafting                          65       700    2.1 lakh  COO (kickoff)

not now (a failed gate is a reason, not a score):
      C8   Replace the Excel tracker                     80       560    1.7 lakh
           - no operator who wants it: the people whose day changes have not asked for it
      C3   KYC document checks and periodic re-KYC       63       900    2.7 lakh
           - no operator who wants it: the people whose day changes have not asked for it
      C6   Collections call summaries                    62     1,440    4.3 lakh
           - no data owner: nobody inside the customer can hand over the inputs
           - no operator who wants it: the people whose day changes have not asked for it
      C5   Branch chatbot for policy questions           55       200    0.6 lakh
           - no baseline: nobody can say today how long it takes or how often it fails
           - no data owner: nobody inside the customer can hand over the inputs
           - no operator who wants it: the people whose day changes have not asked for it
      C4   Title-deed review for secured loans           47       360    1.1 lakh
           - no baseline: nobody can say today how long it takes or how often it fails
           - no data owner: nobody inside the customer can hand over the inputs
           - no operator who wants it: the people whose day changes have not asked for it

consistency warnings (the scores must agree with the evidence):
  - C2: pain 4 but 675 h/month is below the median 688: the pain must come from something other than hours (waiting days, errors, duplicated work); say what in the memo
  - C8: pain 4 but 560 h/month is below the median 688: the pain must come from something other than hours (waiting days, errors, duplicated work); say what in the memo

sensitivity: who wins under other weightings?
  equal weights          C1
  ignore frequency       C1
  ignore pain            C1
  ignore measurability   C1
  ignore data            C1
  ignore safety          C1
  ignore sponsorship     C1
  triple frequency       C1
  triple pain            C1
  triple measurability   C1
  triple data            C1
  triple safety          C1
  triple sponsorship     C1
  winner C1 is stable under every alternative

how to read: score = weighted sum of six 1-to-5 scores, scaled to 100; weights frequency 2, pain 3, measurability 2, data 2, safety 1, sponsorship 2; safety = 6 - blast radius; rupees at 300/hour (planning placeholder)
wrote data/godavari-candidates-ranking.md
```

Four things to take from the run:

1. **Three of eight pass the gates.** The gates did more work than the scores: five candidates
   were declined for a reason that is a sentence ("no baseline") before any weight was argued
   about. That is what you want on a Friday with a COO: reasons, not arithmetic.
2. **The winner is stable.** Thirteen weightings, one winner. The sentence for the COO is
   "intake wins however you weight the columns", and the script is the evidence.
3. **The two warnings are both right and both answered.** C2's pain is waiting days; C8's pain is
   duplicated state. Hours understate both. The memo says so in words, which is the script's point:
   it does not fix the score, it makes you defend it.
4. **The highest score outside the ranking is the tracker, at 80.** A sheet that only printed
   passing candidates would have hidden it. The memo's most important paragraph is about a row the
   script declined.

The ranking table it writes is the one that goes into the memo and, later, the discovery document:

| Rank | Code | Candidate | Score /100 | Freq | Pain | Meas | Data | Safety | Spons | Hours/month | Rupees/month |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | C1 | Loan-file intake and completeness check | 93 | 5 | 5 | 5 | 4 | 3 | 5 | 2,100 | 6.3 lakh |
| 2 | C2 | Income verification from bank statements | 67 | 4 | 4 | 3 | 3 | 2 | 3 | 675 | 2.0 lakh |
| 3 | C7 | Credit-memo drafting | 65 | 5 | 3 | 3 | 3 | 2 | 3 | 700 | 2.1 lakh |

The script also refuses bad input usefully. On a header-only file:

```text
empty.csv: no candidate rows; fill the catalogue first
```

And on a blast radius typed as 6:

```text
C7: 'blast_radius' must be 1 to 5, got 6
```

## 8.4 Part C: write the memo

Copy the template and fill it in order: the sentence first, because if you cannot write it the
scoring is not finished; then the catalogue and gates; then paste the ranking and write the
one-line reason per row in your own words; then the four reasons for the winner, each tied to a
column and carrying a number; then "not now, because, and instead" for every other row; then the
trap check. Section 8 of the template, "what this memo is not", is there so that the COO does not
read a scope or a promise into it.

The filled Godavari memo is the chapter's answer. Read it after yours, and compare three things in
particular: whether your sentence has all four parts and no solution word; whether your "instead"
column gives the COO something real for each declined candidate; and what you did with the
tracker. The memo's answer, that the tracker is folded into intake as a constraint (read it before
replacing it; write status back), is the one Day 7's inputs map and Day 8's plan carry forward.
If you dropped it or picked it, the reasoning in section 5 of the memo is the correction.

## 8.6 Build it with AI

An FDE in 2026 uses an agent for this work in two ways: to draft the catalogue and the memo from
the notes, and to argue against the pick. The script itself is routine (an agent writes it
correctly in one pass). The judgement is where the agent's help is worth having and where its
mistakes are expensive.

**Context to provide:** the Day 0 notes (the tracker, the 6 pm batch, fourteen documents, Lakshmi),
the COO's list with who raised what and when, Thursday's counts, the monthly report's numbers, the
six anchors from section 04, the three gates, and the rule that no model, database, framework or
product-category word may appear in the memo. Say which numbers are counts and which are
estimates.

**A strong prompt (the sceptical reviewer, not the author):**

> Here is my scored catalogue for a lender's candidate problems, the memo I wrote from it, and the
> discovery notes both were built from. Do not rewrite either. Review them as a sceptical FDE lead
> would: (1) for every score of 4 or 5, cite the line in the notes that supports it, or say it is
> unsupported; (2) for every gate marked yes, name the person or report in the notes that makes it
> yes; (3) for each of the five traps (demo, chatbot, sponsor's pet project, no baseline, edge case),
> say which row it applies to and whether the memo caught it; (4) find any number in the memo that
> is not in the notes; (5) find any solution word in the problem statement or the memo; (6) tell me
> what the sponsor will say when he hears the "not now" lines, and whether each "instead" is
> something he can actually do. Do not invent any count, system, person or report that is not in the
> notes.

**A second prompt (the sponsor):**

> Act as the COO of a lender who brought seven problems and has just been told the engagement will
> start with one. You wanted the chatbot. Push back three times, each time with a different
> argument (the board saw it; the analysts' problem is boring; a competitor has one). Score my
> answers on whether I gave a number, a reason, and an alternative you could act on.

**What the agent typically gets right:** the vocabulary of both filters; a complete table with
eight rows and plausible notes; the script; playing the sponsor convincingly; finding a solution
word you missed.

**What it typically gets wrong for this concept (check each):**

| Mistake | How it shows | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **scores the sponsor's enthusiasm as evidence** | the chatbot at frequency 5 and pain 5 on "an estimated 12,000 policy questions a month" that no one counted; sponsorship 5 because "the COO asked for it" | every 4 or 5 cites a count or a report; the sponsorship anchor needs an operator and product fit, not just a sponsor |
| **invents the baseline** | "we will instrument the assistant from day one and have full usage analytics within a week"; measurability 4 for a thing with no count today | a baseline is a number the customer has before you build; "we will measure it afterwards" is the no-baseline trap in new words |
| **puts the solution in the problem statement** | "deploy an LLM-powered assistant over ... to reduce turnaround" | the four-part sentence: unit, cost as the customer's number, cause as the floor sees it, no solution. Search the draft for model, assistant, chatbot, RAG, platform |
| **treats the data owner as a formality** | "we will obtain the recordings from the dialler vendor, which should be straightforward"; data 4 for recordings a third party holds | the owner gate: a person inside the customer who can hand it over this month; a vendor contract is a no |
| **picks two, or seven in phases** | "the assistant in weeks one to six and intake in weeks four to twelve", plus a twelve-month programme | one problem for the twelve weeks; the ranking keeps the others visible; Day 8 decides the 90-day scope |
| **uses the published threshold literally** | "USD 2.4 million a year clears OpenAI's tens-of-millions threshold once the programme is complete" | scale the threshold to the vendor (Day 3): the problem must cost the customer clearly more than the subscription; and never price Indian analyst hours at a US posting median |
| **scores blast radius as "takes no actions"** | the chatbot at risk 1 because "it only answers questions" | a wrong policy answer given to a branch is a wrong loan; blast radius is about the consequence of being wrong, not whether the system writes to a database |
| **treats the tracker as a quick win** | "the Excel tracker replacement as a separate quick win in weeks two to three since the analysts clearly need a better tool" | the analysts did not ask for a replacement; the tracker is a constraint on intake. Read Day 0 again: forty people keep it alive by hand because it is the only true state |
| **agrees with you** | asked to review your memo, it praises the structure and suggests two cosmetic changes | give it the anchors and the gates as the rubric; ask it to argue for the second candidate; ask what the compliance head would say |

The two to watch most are the first and the third. An agent will produce a well-formatted sheet in
which the sponsor's favourite scores highest, and a confident problem statement that names the
product. Both read as competence. Both are the engagement lost in week one.

## 8.7 How do you know it's right?

Whether a human or an agent produced the catalogue and the memo, the checks are the same:

1. **Every 4 or 5 has a source.** For each high score, point to the count, the report or the
   person. A score with no source is a 3 at most until Day 6 or Day 9 supplies one.
2. **Every "yes" gate names a person or a report.** "Baseline: the monthly report's turnaround and
   send-back rate." "Owner: the Vijayawada team lead, for the tracker." "Operator: Srinivas, on
   Monday, unprompted." A gate without a name is a no.
3. **The script runs clean, and every warning has an answer in the memo.** A warning left
   unanswered means the score and the evidence disagree and you have not decided which is right.
4. **The winner is stable, or the memo says it is a judgement call.** If the sensitivity line
   reports changes, section 4 of the memo must say under which weighting the second candidate wins
   and why you still chose the first.
5. **The sentence has four parts and no solution word.** `grep -n -i "model\|llm\|assistant\|chatbot\|rag\|platform\|automat"`
   over the memo should return nothing, or only quotations of what the COO said.
6. **Every declined candidate has an "instead" the sponsor can act on.** "Count the calls for a
   month" is actionable. "Revisit later" is not.
7. **The traps table has a row for each of the five**, and the rows that say "none" have a
   sentence saying what you looked for.
8. **The tracker is neither picked nor dropped.** If your memo does either, re-read Day 0.
9. **The hours reconcile with the engagement's known numbers.** 1,400 files times 4.5 hours is
   about 6,300 analyst hours a month across forty analysts, about 160 hours each, which is a full
   month. If your volume or hours imply more than that, one of them is wrong.
10. **The COO could read it in twenty minutes and sign the sentence.** Read it aloud with a timer.

**Review checklist:**

- [ ] eight rows (seven from the room, one from the floor); the CRM absent because it was declined on Day 3
- [ ] every volume and hours figure has a source or is marked as a guess
- [ ] scores against the written anchors; every 4 or 5 sourced
- [ ] three gates answered with a name or a report, not "probably"
- [ ] script runs clean; warnings answered in words; sensitivity line quoted
- [ ] the sentence: unit of work, cost as the customer's number, cause as the floor sees it, no solution; signed by the sponsor and two operator leads
- [ ] four reasons for the winner, each tied to a column, each with a number
- [ ] "not now, because, and instead" for every other row, in ranking order
- [ ] the trap check filled; the tracker folded into the pick as a constraint
- [ ] "what this memo is not" present; the rupee rate marked as a placeholder until Day 9

## 8.8 Extend it

- Add a `depends_on` column (C7 depends on C1; C2 partly on C1) and make the script warn when a
  candidate ranks above one it depends on. Does the ranking need it at Godavari?
- Change `WEIGHTS` so that safety is 3 and pain is 1, rerun, and read the sensitivity line. Then
  write the paragraph you would need to defend that weighting to the COO, and notice how hard it is.
- Add a ninth row for a candidate the COO did not raise and the floor did not show: "summarise the
  internal audit reports". Score it honestly and watch where the edge-case trap lands.
- Rerun with `--rate 450` and check that nothing in the ranking moves and everything in the rupee
  column does. That is the point of keeping the rate out of the score.
