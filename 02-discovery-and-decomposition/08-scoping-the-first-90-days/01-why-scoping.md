## 1.1 Friday of week two

It is Friday, 6 November 2026, the end of discovery week two at Godavari Finance. On your laptop is
Day 7's output: six workstreams in an order you can defend, each with a suggested start week. The
COO has twenty minutes at four o'clock. He will ask one question, the one every sponsor asks at this
point: "So when do my analysts get it?"

There are two ways to answer.

The first is Day 7's week grid on a slide. It is honest and useless to him: week numbers mean nothing
on his calendar, it ignores the freeze his core-system owner enforces from 17 December, it gives him no
date to decide anything on, and it does not mention the branch CRM, which he therefore assumes is coming.

The second is one page. Across the top, real dates: day 1 was Monday 26 October, the day-90 review is
Friday 22 January. Four dates on which he decides something, each
with its question. Twenty-two milestones, each a sentence he could read to his board. The freeze, Sankranti and the RBI
inspection drawn on the chart in red, with the plan routed around them. Who does what on both sides,
with one name accountable for each line. And at the bottom, four lines under the heading "We will not
do these", each with a reason, waiting for his signature.

The first answer is a schedule. The second is a **scope**: a commitment about what will happen, when,
by whom, and what will not happen at all. This chapter teaches how to build the second one.

## 1.2 Why it matters for your career

Two facts from the Part 2 plan and the course's market research (sources in section 12):

- **Day 90 is where most engagements are judged.** The FDE playbooks frame the first engagement around
  a 30-60-90 shape, and Atlassian's playbook sets the bar at about twelve weeks to a self-sufficient
  customer team (Day 1). Twelve weeks is 84 days. If nothing is in daily use by then, the sponsor's
  confidence has usually run out, and the renewal conversation starts with "what did we get?"
- **An unscoped engagement becomes the services trap.** Day 3 showed the arithmetic: saying yes to the
  branch CRM keeps an FDE on site full time for 36 months and drops revenue over FDE cost from a healthy
  multiple to 2.2x, below the floor. The trap is never one big yes. It is a series of small ones that
  nobody wrote down a refusal for.

The interview loop tests the same thing: the decomposition round expects you to sequence the work and
name what you would not do (Day 7), and the capstone defence ([Day 46](../../08-capstone-and-career/46-capstone-defence-and-career/README.md))
asks for a 30/60/90 plan out loud. Customer-facing discovery is in 70 percent or more of the 1,000 FDE
postings analysed in 2026 and rising fastest, and scoping is the part of it a hiring manager can check:
a plan either has dates, owners and a "will not do" list or it does not.

> **Analogy (Telugu audience).** Planning a wedding. The hall is booked first, because good halls are
> scarce and the muhurtham date cannot move. The caterer comes next. The invitations are printed last,
> because they are easy and because printing them before the hall is confirmed means printing them
> twice. Nobody plans a wedding in "week numbers". They plan it against the panchangam, the exam dates
> of the cousins, and the month the priest is unavailable. A 90-day plan is the same: the scarce,
> risky things first, the easy things last, and every date checked against a calendar you do not
> control.

## 1.3 What scoping is, in plain words

Scoping turns an ordered list of workstreams into five things a customer can sign:

1. a **dated plan**: phases that overlap on purpose, each workstream with a real start and end date;
2. **milestones a COO can read**, each with an owner on both sides, and **checkpoints** where the
   customer answers a question that decides what happens next;
3. a **two-company RACI**: for every workstream, exactly one person accountable, and somebody at the
   customer responsible for anything that must keep running after you leave;
4. a **"will not do" list**: the things the sponsor or someone else wanted that this engagement will
   not build, each with a reason and the name of the person who agreed;
5. **buffers and a slip order**: where the slack is, and what gets cut first when a date you do not
   control moves.

Day 7 gave you the order and the reasons. Scoping adds time, people and refusals. Nothing in this
chapter re-opens the decomposition; if you find yourself rescoring risk, go back to
[Day 7](../07-decomposition/README.md).

## 1.5 The hard parts, named up front

1. **Dates feel like promises, and they are.** Week numbers are comfortable because nobody can hold you
   to them. Dates can be held against you. That is exactly why the customer needs them, and why every
   date in this chapter comes with an owner, a dependency and a buffer.
2. **The long pole is not yours.** The security review, the access tickets, the release freeze and the
   RBI inspection are on other people's calendars. A plan that treats them as tasks you will finish is
   fiction. Section 03 treats them as constraints you route around.
3. **Saying what you will not do feels rude.** It is the opposite. The unwritten refusal is the one
   that gets reopened every Friday. Section 05 shows why the written one protects the relationship.
4. **The plan will be wrong by week three.** Something will slip; on Godavari it is likely to be the
   sandbox or the review. The skill is not a perfect plan. It is a plan that says in advance what moves
   first, so that a slip is a decision and not a crisis (section 06).

By the end you will have dated Godavari's 90 days from Day 7's real output, checked them against the
customer's calendar, run three slips, and written the page the COO signs.
