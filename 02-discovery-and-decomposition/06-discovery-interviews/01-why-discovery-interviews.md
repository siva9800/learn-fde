## 1.1 Wednesday, Vijayawada, 09:40

You have the SOP. It was emailed to you on Monday by the COO's office: "Loan Origination Standard Operating Procedure, v3, 2019", twelve numbered steps, two pages, a flowchart with rounded boxes. It is a good document. It is also, as you find out at 09:40 on Wednesday sitting beside Lakshmi, a description of a process that nobody in the building follows.

Step one of the SOP is "open the application in the core lending system". Lakshmi's first act of the day is to open an Excel workbook on a shared drive. The SOP's step three is "notify the branch of missing documents". Lakshmi's version is a phone number from a notebook, a four-minute call, a line in the spreadsheet that says "waiting on address proof since", and the row coloured red. The SOP has no step for the passbook page that arrives on her personal WhatsApp from a branch officer in Guntur, which she prints, staples to the paper file and notes as "received WA". By 11:35 you have watched one file reach "waiting" and you have counted nineteen things she did, seven of which exist in no document.

That afternoon you ask her the question you now know to ask: "The SOP has twelve steps. How many are there really?" She laughs. "If you want to know the steps, watch the tracker. The tracker is the process."

This chapter is about the four or five days of the engagement in which you find that out, on purpose, before any production code exists. It covers getting real access (and what to do when you do not get it), shadowing, planning and running 8 to 10 interviews across the people on [Day 5](../05-the-stakeholder-map/README.md)'s map, asking without leading, writing down what was said rather than what you concluded, drawing the workflow as it actually runs, and reading it back to the people who gave it to you. And it is about one category of question that matters more than the rest: the ones whose answers change the architecture.

## 1.2 Why it matters for your career

Three reasons, from the research behind this course (CURRICULUM.md and the Part 2 plan; sources in section 16):

- **One answer in one interview can change the retrieval architecture, and finding it in week eight costs a rewrite.** At Godavari the answer is that branch managers must not see other branches' files, and that the rule is enforced by Active Directory groups synced nightly from HR. Heard in week two, it is a requirement for Day 15's retrieval and Day 22's identity work. Heard in month three, after a vector index has been built without the user's groups in every query, it is a month of rework and a CISO who now believes you did not ask. Every engagement has one of these. The job is to find it early.
- **The teams that invented the role interview before they build.** OpenAI's forward deployed team describes involving the customer's experts before serious development and treating the eval set agreed with them as the contract. The 2026 discovery playbooks say "interview at least 8 to 10 end users, not just the executive sponsor", ask about "the last time the process failed" rather than abstract requirements, and treat a pain mentioned once as a hypothesis and one mentioned in three independent interviews as a requirement. Atlassian's playbook frames the whole job as mapping "who is involved, which systems hold the necessary knowledge, where handoffs break down". Hiring managers who have read those playbooks ask "how did you run discovery?" and listen for shadowing, a count, and a finding that changed the design.
- **It is the raw material of the round with the lowest pass rate.** [Day 7](../07-decomposition/README.md)'s decomposition method has a step called "map the inputs: shape, owner, freshness, access, what is missing". In the interview you reason about it from the prompt; on the job you fill it from this week. A candidate who has actually sat beside an operator describes inputs differently: she says "I would expect an unofficial tracker" and "I would ask who may see what" without being prompted, because she has been burned. Customer-facing discovery appears in 70 percent or more of the 1,000 FDE postings analysed in 2026 and is the fastest-rising of the three buckets ([Day 2](../../01-the-role/02-the-three-buckets/README.md)); interviewing and shadowing are its most concrete skills.

> **Analogy (Telugu audience).** Google Maps versus the auto driver. The sponsor hands you the map: twelve steps, rounded boxes, the official route. The auto driver knows the lane that floods in August, the one-way that nobody follows after nine, the gate that is locked after eight and the chai shop where the branch officer waits for the courier. Both are useful. Only one of them gets you there on a Tuesday. Interview the auto drivers. Then draw their map, and show it to them before you show it to anyone else.

## 1.4 The hard parts, named up front

1. **You will not have credentials, and it will feel like you cannot start.** The CISO said no, no and not yet on Tuesday. Section 02 shows that the most valuable discovery work needs a chair, a notebook and permission to watch, and that the access tickets you raise on day one are the clock you start on everything else.
2. **Interviewing feels like a soft skill and is a technical one.** A leading question ("would you use a tool that typed the fields for you?") produces a yes from every operator in India and tells you nothing. An open one ("show me the last one you did") takes longer, produces silence you have to sit through, and gives you the nineteen steps. Sections 05 and 06 teach the mechanics: what to ask, what never to ask, how to take notes that are evidence rather than memory.
3. **Watching is slower than asking and is worth more.** It finds what no interview does, and it builds the trust that makes the interview honest: an operator who has seen you count her clicks without judging her will tell you what she skips on a bad day. Section 03.
4. **The finding that matters will sound boring.** "Visibility is by AD group" is a sentence an admin says in passing. "The batch runs at six" is a fact everyone in the building knows. Neither sounds like architecture. Section 08 trains you to hear them.
5. **You will be tempted to let an assistant write the findings.** It will write confident, well-structured findings that nobody said. Section 12's Build it with AI covers the specific failure and the script that catches part of it.

By the end of the chapter you will have a discovery pack in the engagement folder: an interview plan built from the map, persona question banks, a shadowing log, ten coded transcripts, a nineteen-step situational map in Mermaid reviewed with the operators, a synthesis with the architecture-changing findings called out, and a script that tallies the coded findings and refuses a synthesis that cites a finding no interviewee gave.
