A chapter about a week has production concerns too: what happens to the 60/30/10 shape when the
engagement is under pressure, and how to notice. The symptoms below show up in the calendar before they
show up anywhere else, which is why the calendar is the instrument.

## 7.1 The week that goes 90/10/0

In week six, with the walking skeleton late and the COO asking for a date, the calendar fills with
meetings: status calls, escalation calls, a workshop the sponsor asked for, a demo for a board member.
The deployment slice shrinks to a few hours stolen at night and the internal slice disappears. Each
meeting is defensible; the week is not. The sign to watch is not the 90; it is the **0**. A fortnight
with no hours for product means the engagement has stopped generating patterns, which is the moment the
FDE model quietly becomes consulting (Day 3's trap, Day 38's dark ages). The fix is mechanical: the
calendar keeps a protected block for the pattern memo, and if it is cancelled twice, the engagement
lead hears about it.

## 7.2 The week that goes 10/90/0

The opposite failure, usually in weeks eight to ten: the integration is hard, the engineer goes quiet,
and the customer hears nothing for two weeks. The COO's first question in the next readout is "what
have you been doing?", and the honest answer ("fighting your SOAP API") lands badly because nobody saw
the fight. Day 30 teaches the conversation; the calendar prevents the need for it by keeping the Friday
readout hour and at least one shadowing or review hour every week, however hard the code is. An
engineer who disappears into bucket 1 has also stopped learning what to build.

## 7.3 Context switching

Twelve people in two weeks, three buckets in one day, and a laptop that moves from the credit-ops floor
to the train to a sandbox. The cost is real and the published accounts do not pretend otherwise. What
helps, in order of how much: keep whole mornings or afternoons in one bucket where the calendar allows
(the sample fortnight does this on most days); write the shadowing notes on the same day, because
bucket-3 input decays overnight; keep the engagement log as the one place where facts from all three
buckets land, so Friday's readout is assembled, not remembered.

## 7.4 Travel, access and the hours that are not in the table

The Vijayawada day is seven counted hours and about five uncounted ones on trains. The CISO's no on
Tuesday means the sandbox spike waits until a test login exists. Neither is in the split, and both decide
whether the split is achievable. Plan travel as its own line, not as customer-facing time, and treat
access as a workstream with a start date (Day 7's step 4 scores it; Day 23 runs it). A calendar that
assumes credentials on Monday is the most common way a week-one plan fails.

## 7.5 Cost, in the plain sense

An FDE's hours are the vendor's most expensive line (Day 3 prices them; Ramp's measure is enterprise
revenue over FDE salaries). A fortnight of 70 hours at 60/28/11 is a product decision as much as a
schedule: the 42 customer-facing hours are the ones that make the patterns possible, and the 8 internal
hours are the ones that make them pay for more than one customer. When the engagement lead asks "can
you do this remotely and halve the customer-facing time?", the honest answer in week one is "not without
missing the tracker", and the honest answer in week nine may be yes. The split is not fixed across the
engagement, and the calendar is where you show which weeks need presence.

## 7.6 Key takeaways

- Watch the zero, not the ninety: a fortnight with no internal hours means no patterns, which is the
  services trap starting.
- Never a week without a readout hour and a customer hour, however hard the code.
- Batch by bucket within a day; write bucket-3 notes the same day; one engagement log.
- Travel and access are not in the split and decide whether it is achievable; plan both as lines.
- The split moves across the engagement; the calendar is how you show which weeks need presence.
